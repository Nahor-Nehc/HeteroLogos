2. Where do we get data for the remaining phonemes?

This is the difficult part.

Unfortunately, there is no single complete, high-quality English confusion matrix covering all 39 CMU/ARPABET phonemes that's as widely used as Miller & Nicely for consonants.

Researchers have instead accumulated data across different subsets and tasks.

Vowels

Several classic studies measured vowel confusions, including work by D. B. Fry and colleagues, as well as later experiments by James Hillenbrand and collaborators on vowel identification. These datasets tend to focus on subsets of English vowels under various listening conditions rather than the full inventory.

TIMIT recognition results

Many speech-recognition papers publish confusion matrices on the TIMIT corpus.

These are machine confusions, not human confusions.

They're useful as a secondary source but shouldn't be treated as perceptual ground truth.

Luce's Neighborhood work

Paul Luce and colleagues compiled extensive perceptual similarity data for spoken words.

These don't directly provide phoneme-to-phoneme matrices, but they have been used to derive phoneme similarity measures.

Phonological CorpusTools

The software includes several published similarity metrics, although these are usually feature-based rather than directly derived from confusion experiments.

Modern behavioural studies

Recent studies continue to publish confusion matrices for particular consonant or vowel inventories, often as supplementary CSV files.

These are valuable, but they're fragmented:

different speaker populations,
different noise levels,
different phoneme subsets,
different experimental designs.

There's no universally accepted merged resource.

3. A better idea than trying to find a complete confusion matrix

Rather than searching for a nonexistent perfect dataset, I'd recommend treating the missing data as an estimation problem.

You already have:

a complete SPE feature matrix,
reliable human consonant confusions,
theoretical knowledge about vowels.

You could:

Learn weights from consonants.
Check whether the learned weights produce sensible vowel neighbourhoods (e.g. /IY/ close to /IH/, /AA/ close to /AO/).
Validate against published vowel confusion studies where available.
Adjust only if there is systematic disagreement.

This is much closer to how many statistical models in linguistics are developed: fit on the richest available data, then test generalisation.

4. If you wanted the strongest possible model

One approach that I don't think has been fully explored in the literature is to combine three sources of evidence into a single optimisation:

Human confusion data (the primary target).
Distinctive features (the explanatory representation).
Acoustic distances computed from many recordings of each phoneme (for example, MFCC-based distances averaged over multiple speakers).

The model would learn feature weights that jointly explain both perceptual confusions and acoustic similarity. That could help in areas where behavioural data are sparse—particularly vowels—without replacing human data where they exist.

My recommendation

For a research project, I would proceed in stages:

Build a transparent baseline using weighted SPE features.
Fit the weights with maximum-likelihood estimation on the available English consonant confusion data (Miller & Nicely and other published consonant studies).
Validate the model against independent vowel confusion datasets instead of using them for training.
Only then consider adding acoustic information if you need better coverage or predictive accuracy.

That progression keeps the model interpretable and grounded in behavioural evidence, while making the best use of the limited perceptual datasets that are currently available.
